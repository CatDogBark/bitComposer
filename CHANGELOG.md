# Changelog

## Unreleased

- `docs/drivers/snes/README.md` lists where the built-in style packs' habits, measured from snapshot registers before the emulator existed, differ from what the drivers do in play: FFVI's echo feedback is chosen per song (0.78 most often, not 0.64 everywhere); Secret of Mana's echo comes in two kinds, short and dry or long and ringing, not one at 0.31 feedback; Secret of Evermore's echo is on in 60 of 72 songs, not off in 47, and its driver writes every note's envelope; Mega Man X's notes are released by a GAIN fade and its melody doubled 0.12 s late. The packs are unchanged.
- `docs/drivers/`: every game the SPC import is tested against, by console and music driver (a file per driver family, so a new game edits one file and a new console gets a folder): composer (as the tags credit), what the driver does with the SNES chip (its clock, envelopes, pitch writes, pitch modulation, noise, echo, master volume, density; measured over each song's first minute), the conversion scores, what the converter carries for it, what it means for generation, known limits and open findings. Four drivers so far (Square's AKAO in FFVI, Secret of Mana's, Secret of Evermore's, Capcom's in Mega Man X), 258 songs. `tools/spc_check.py traits` measures the drivers' habits it is written from. It records too that the style packs' echo habits, read from snapshot registers, are not what the drivers set in play.
- SPC to IT: each echo repeat plays a copy of its sample as the echo hands it back after that many trips round its loop: the echo filter applied once a trip, its colour and its span. A trip comes the echo delay and the filter's span late; on rows alone the repeats of FFVI's Dark World (one tap, 7 samples back) came 7 samples a trip early, stacked differently on its held organ chords and sounded a third brighter and up to 15 % louder than the original, heard as the organ going up in pitch. Now the organ's echo brightness is within a few percent of the original's and its loudness within 3 %. The copies take what samples and instruments IT has left after the pitch-modulation recordings; later repeats play the last.
- Development: `tools/spc_check.py` reads its loudness contours and RMS from every sample. A stride lands on the same points of a loop whose period it divides, and BRR loops of 8 or 16 samples are common: a softened square read every fourth sample measured 4 dB low.
- Development: `tools/spc_check.py` measures SPC-to-IT conversions against the emulator, the whole mix and voice by voice (the emulator playing one voice alone, the module that voice's channels): loudness over time and its gap in dB, level, tone (the averaged log spectra, where a wrong timbre at the right loudness shows), and a voice's echo against its dry sound (the original's taken apart with the voice's tapped output). `sweep` converts every SPC file in a folder of game folders in parallel and `report` summarises it by music driver. The voice-by-voice measures found the SPC-to-IT fixes listed below, and a measure that only looks at the whole mix would have passed The Phantom Train's beep.
- SPC to IT ends a song as an SPC player does: the tag's length, then its fade (most songs loop), the fade written as global volume, and the module ends on the song's last row. Patterns were rounded up to a whole one, so notes still held at the end rang on for up to two seconds past it with their echo stacked on: FFVI's Dark World ran twice as loud past its end. `convert(snap, seconds, fade=)` fades a given length.
- SPC to IT: the echo rings out as long as the original's and at its strength. Repeats go on until they fall to -30 dB (FFVI's Dark World, feedback 0.78 at 80 ms, rings for about 1.1 s; three repeats stopped at 0.24 s), on channels shared among only the voices that send, within IT's 64. The SNES chip leaves the echo out of the master volume (output = dry x master + echo x echo volume), while the IT global volume reaches every channel, so the repeats are raised by the master volume at their time: at FFVI's 63 of 128 the echo came out at half its strength. Dark World's echo against its dry sound: 0.91 to 1.03, the original's 0.85 to 1.06 (was 0.40 to 0.46); FFVI's median level 0.91 to 0.98 over its 79 songs.
- SPC to IT plays samples and envelopes as the SNES chip does. The SNES chip reads every sample through a 4-point Gaussian interpolation, a lowpass in the sample's own terms (-4 dB at a quarter of its rate, -15 dB at the top), while an IT player interpolates almost ideally; each sample is now filtered with that response first (9 taps, within 0.4 dB of it, computed from the hardware table). Envelope steps rewritten while a note sounds now fall where the SNES chip's shared rate counter puts them, not counted from the note's start: Secret of Evermore's driver shapes its notes with hundreds of GAIN ramps and holds each, and drifted a step on most. Its wind (Village on the Plateau) was 1.7 to 2.2 times too loud and the wrong tone, from tone 0.50 to 0.99 against the emulator and level 2.17 to 0.95; an ordinary instrument's top octave from 1.56 times too strong to 0.91 (Secret of Mana). Across the 1,727 voices that matter in the four test games' 258 songs, the tenth percentile of tone went from 0.96 to 0.98 (median 0.99).
- SPC to IT: a note shorter than half a row (under 5 ms at 100 rows a second) now takes its row and is cut on the next. It was never cut, so it rang on to the voice's next note, and when its envelope was rewritten too the conversion failed (two of Mega Man X's 35 songs, Armored Armadillo and Sigma Fortress 2, found by converting all 258 songs of the four test games and measuring each against the emulator).
- The vision (`VISION.md`): a composition engine with its own research pipeline. Game music reconstructed through emulation into IT, measured, and turned into generation resources (measurements, never melodies or samples); the generator's own songs measured with the same tools; the listener's keeps closing the loop. With where it stands now, next and later. The roadmap points to it.
- SPC to IT keeps pitch modulation and plays at the original's level. A note whose pitch the voice below bends (PMON: an FM timbre, or a tone shaken into a hiss by a noise voice, as in FFVI's The Phantom Train, which played a steady beep) is kept as the emulator played it, the voice's own output recorded through the note; notes played alike share one recording, on the keyboard of an instrument of recordings (`it_format.ITInstrument.keyboard`). Volumes go side for side (an IT player pans linearly, so centred notes played at half their level) with the SNES chip's master volume as IT global volume: Secret of Mana's songs, at half, now play at 0.98 of the emulator's level. Noise is the SNES chip's own, its shift register rendered at the output rate for each noise clock, held as the hardware holds it. A note whose envelope is rewritten while it sounds carries its whole envelope in the volume column (Secret of Evermore's Southern Jungle wind was silent: 0.56 to 0.94). Envelope ticks hold RMS, not mean. Loudness contours against the emulator, per side: 0.84 to 0.98 across eight songs of the four test games.
- The emulator 0.2.0: `run(..., mute=, taps=)` leaves voices out of the mixed output (to compare one voice at a time against a conversion) and records chosen voices' own output (after the envelope, before the volumes). `bitcomposer.spc` needs 0.2.0 and says so when an older one is installed.
- SPC to IT carries the echo, the master volume and envelope changes. Each note sending to the echo plays again on a channel of its own, an echo delay late, up to three times, from a copy of its sample coloured by the echo filter and scaled by the echo volume, the feedback and the filter's gain over the note itself; echo volumes of opposite signs play in surround. The SNES chip's master volume (a driver's fade-out) becomes IT global volume. Envelope registers written while a note sounds (Mega Man X's driver releases every note with a GAIN fade, not a key-off) scale its volume and cut it where it falls silent: Spark Mandrill's melody, measured voice by voice against the emulator, went from 0.54 to 0.94. A note keyed silent and swelled up afterwards is no longer dropped (Mega Man X's lead). Loudness contours against the emulator, per side: FFVI 0.97, Secret of Mana 0.91 to 0.93, Secret of Evermore 0.96 (Southern Jungle 0.56), Mega Man X 0.80 to 0.91.
- SPC to IT (`bitcomposer/spc/convert.py`; `python -m bitcomposer.spc --to-it OUT.it FILE.spc`): a game's song as an IT module of its own samples and the notes its driver played, in IT instrument mode with each sample and envelope setting an instrument (volume envelopes simulated from ADSR or GAIN), bends as fine slides, rows on the driver's tick where its key-ons sit on one (else 100 rows a second). Samples are found by the addresses the SNES chip read at each key-on, which the emulator now records, since many drivers fill in a voice's directory entry just before keying it.
- IT instruments in the writer (`it_format.ITInstrument`, `write_it_file(instruments=...)`): instrument mode with a volume envelope per instrument, for the SPC converter. Without instruments a file is written exactly as before: 72 songs across every sound are byte-identical.
- SPC notes: the emulator called from Python (`bitcomposer_emu.run`; `bitcomposer/spc/emulate.py`), the SNES chip decoder that replays a driver's writes into notes (`bitcomposer/spc/notes.py`: voice, sample, pitch, volume and envelope at key-on, bends and swells, the end), and each sample's own pitch measured (`bitcomposer/spc/tuning.py`) so notes have names. `python -m bitcomposer.spc --notes FILE.spc` lists the notes a driver plays. Tests use a seven-byte hand-written driver and synthetic tones, and skip the emulator where it is not installed.
- The emulator's SNES chip (`emu/src/dsp.rs`), written from fullsnes: the voices (BRR, Gaussian interpolation, pitch modulation, noise), the ADSR and GAIN envelopes, KON and KOFF timing, ENDX, the echo with its FIR filter and feedback, and the mixer, one sample every 32 CPU cycles alongside the CPU (`spc::run`), so drivers read live state. Against libgme's rendering of the same snapshots, FFVI's battle theme correlates at 0.98 and Secret of Mana's at 0.93; Secret of Evermore's noise-heavy songs cannot line up sample for sample (the noise sequence's position is not in an SPC file), and listened to side by side all four sound identical. About 200 to 230 times real time with the output on. `run_spc --wav out.wav` writes it.
- The emulator's SNES sound CPU, in Rust (`emu/src/cpu.rs`): every instruction, cycle-counted, passing all 254,000 cases of the SingleStepTests suite (`SPC700_TESTS=... cargo test`). Around it the SNES sound unit (`emu/src/spc.rs`): RAM, the I/O registers, the three timers, the boot ROM, the SNES chip as a register file, and a log of every write the driver makes to it, loaded from an SPC snapshot. Real songs from the four test games run at about 1,000 to 1,180 times real time with logs identical to the Python prototype's. `cargo run --release --example run_spc -- song.spc` shows what a driver did.
- Development: the emulator's package skeleton, `emu/` (Rust crate `bitcomposer-emu`, the Python module `bitcomposer_emu` when built with maturin). The emulator is plain Rust and `cargo test` needs no Python; the wheel turns the binding on, as an abi3 wheel serving every Python from 3.10. CONTRIBUTING says how to build it.
- SPC snapshots read (`bitcomposer/spc/`, step 2 of the SPC import plan): the file, its ID666 tag (text or binary), the DSP registers (per voice the volume, pitch, sample and envelope; the echo, noise and pitch modulation) and the samples, decoded with the chip's own arithmetic, including the 15-bit wrap the research decoder clipped instead. Loops are stored as they sound on the repeats, since a loop that predicts across its seam decodes differently on its first pass; directory entries that are leftovers in RAM are told from samples by how encoders write them. `python -m bitcomposer.spc FILE.spc` shows what a snapshot holds; `--export DIR` writes every sample of a set of snapshots once as WAV with its loop in `samples.json`. Checked against the research tools over the 258 snapshots the packs came from: 3,414 of 4,728 samples decode to exactly twice their values, and the rest differ only where the research decoder ran past 15 bits. Tests build synthetic snapshots: the repository holds no game files.
- Pack files: a style pack as data. A JSON file names the pack's palette, the voices each part draws from, its habits and its channel trims, and brings its own voices as WAV files (tuned from the recording's note or pitch in Hz, with a loop, a release family and a volume); `bitcomposer --pack-file pack.json` composes with it. Any role or habit left out keeps the palette's, so a pack can change as little as its lead, and a pack can borrow any built-in voice by name. Everything is checked on load, and a mistake is reported with where it is (`habits.tempo.medum: not one of slow, medium, fast`). Data only: a pack file cannot run anything. A pack file is never drawn by a random sound. The built-in packs stay code, since their tables carry comments recording where each number came from, but a test writes each one in the format and reads it back unchanged, so the format can say everything they say. The format is `docs/PACKS.md`; it is the first step of the SPC import plan (`docs/SPC-IMPORT.md`), whose importer will write these files. Built-in sounds are unchanged: 72 songs across every sound and the main options are byte-identical before and after.
- The SPC import plan: the emulator will be Rust, chosen by a prototype. A complete SNES sound CPU core in plain Python passed all 254,000 SingleStepTests cases and ran real songs from the four test games at 4.3 to 5.0 times real time; Secret of Evermore's driver reads the SNES chip's live envelope levels and end flags, so the SNES chip must be emulated too, which brings Python to about 1.5 to 2.5 times real time against about 170 for a compiled emulator. Rust rather than C because SPC files are untrusted input. The emulator will ship as a second, compiled package, optional where no wheel exists.
- The SPC import plan gains what others have built (tests for an SNES sound CPU core, reference emulators, spc2it, VGMTrans) and three decisions: emulation with VGMTrans as the checker, an emulator in whatever language is fast enough (the generator stays pure Python), and research material studied rather than pasted where its license does not fit an MIT project. Its design: a source per file format (SPC through the emulator; VGM already a register log), a chip decoder per sound chip turning register logs into common note events, and one translator shared by every console.
- The SPC import plan (`docs/SPC-IMPORT.md`): a pack made from a game's own `.spc` files, through a reader, an SPC700 emulator that runs the game's music driver and logs what it tells the DSP, and a translator from that log to notes, roles and habits; an SPC-to-IT conversion as the translator's proof; and the player's PACKS panel (beside MIX: the imported packs, IMPORT with a progress screen and a role picker, USE; imported packs never in the random draw). The roadmap: Flathub (KDE Discover, GNOME Software) is for later, when there is an audience; the desktop extras are an ideas list.
- Development: pull requests into `dev` run no checks on GitHub; the full test suite is run locally before each one merges (CONTRIBUTING has the command). GitHub's checks (the tests on every supported Python, the Windows build) run on the pull request that takes `dev` into `main` for a release, on `main`, and on tags, and either can be started by hand on a branch. CONTRIBUTING describes the branches: `main` holds released code, work goes into `dev` one topic per pull request, and the release step is the `dev`-to-`main` pull request.
- Tests: `test_bridge` checked the generating flag after a 0.1 s wait, which a fast machine outran (GitHub's Python 3.12 runner finished a chip song inside it and failed a docs-only pull request); it now checks the flag as `generate()` returns, when it is always up.

## 1.0.1 — 2026-10-18

UPDATE on Linux gets past pip's saved copy of PyPI's version list.

- Updating to 1.0.0 from a candidate failed on a laptop minutes after the release: the player saw 1.0.0 on GitHub, but pip read its saved copy of PyPI's version list, which ended at rc6, and said no such version exists (PyPI itself listed 1.0.0; the same install with pip's cache off found it). Now when pip says the version does not exist, the update asks once more with pip's cache off (`PIP_NO_CACHE_DIR=1`), and if PyPI still does not list it the screen says so and to try again in a few minutes. Updates otherwise keep the cache, so Qt is not downloaded again. Windows was not affected: its update downloads the installer from the GitHub release. The retry is in the updater of the version doing the updating, so it helps from 1.0.1 on; a 1.0.0 player that hits this updates on a later try.

## 1.0.0 — 2026-10-18

The first official release, and bitComposer is now a player: one program on Linux and Windows that composes the next song as the current one ends, with a live mixer, KEEP and the ten-song prune, media keys, start at login and an UPDATE button that shows its work. Install it with `pipx install bitcomposer` or the Windows installer. Six release candidates took it from the first Qt window to an update path proven on both platforms, tested on Plasma, GNOME and Windows. Under the player, the week after 0.9.1 was a release listen across every sound, and what it turned up was mostly engine-wide: the note conversion had every tonal part an octave below its label since the first version, the harmony's lowest voice sat under the bass in every song, a rhythmic harmony mode could land on a pad that never finished its swell, a lone harmony voice was arpeggiated into a rasp on bright voices, songs ran from 42 seconds to six minutes, and the widget's playlist hid every track past the eighth. Each was measured on rendered audio before it was changed, and each change was heard on a page before it merged. Then the harmony itself was reworked: two or three real voices in a register per family, stabs held as long as the voice needs, the level measured. Titles now read the song. The compatibility promise is written down in the README: the same seed on the same version is the same song. Releases go to PyPI from a tag.

- The player, on Linux and Windows. `bitcomposer-player` (Linux) and `bitComposer.exe` (Windows) are one program, in the package next to the generator: a folder of songs through libopenmpt, GENERATE AND PLAY, auto-generate when a track ends, the compose settings with presets, a live mixer with the KIT and ECHO buses and APPLY TO TRACK, gain with the soft limiter, shuffle, KEEP and the ten-track prune, the readout of what the song drew, and an UPDATE button when a newer release exists (pipx reinstall and restart on Linux, the release's installer on Windows; a candidate sees candidates, a release only releases). The engine (`bitcomposer/player/engine.py`) is the Plasma daemon's core lifted out with no D-Bus in it; generation runs in-process. Audio out through the standard library alone: paplay, pw-cat or aplay on Linux, waveOut on Windows. Windows releases carry `bitComposer-setup.exe` (Inno Setup: Start menu, uninstaller) with libopenmpt bundled; `bitComposer.exe` is now the player rather than the generate-only window. `bitcomposer-player --install-desktop` writes the Linux app-menu entry; `--check` reports the library and the audio output. The CLI's option parsing is `cli.build_parser` and one song is `cli.compose_from_args`, so the window's settings and the command line's are one list.
- The player's window is Qt, the Plasma widget's layout, colours and type. The first window was tkinter with a fixed 520×640 minimum and pixel-sized sliders, and at 1920×1080 and 150% scaling (1280×720 logical) it ran off the screen. The window is now the widget's QML on plain QtQuick (`player/qml/Main.qml`) over a QObject that wraps the engine (`player/backend.py`), so state is pushed rather than polled. Sizes are logical pixels; the window sizes itself once, no taller than the screen it is on, and after that the settings and mix panels open inside it, scrolled into view, and a size you drag is kept (growing the window for a panel, as the widget's popup did, ran it off the bottom of a Wayland screen and flashed while it resized); option rows wrap; Ctrl + and Ctrl − zoom, Ctrl 0 resets, and the zoom, the compose settings, the preset and the tab are remembered. Closing it leaves the music playing in the tray, and a second launch shows the first window. JetBrains Mono and the new icon ship in the package. PySide6-Essentials is now a dependency of the package (a clone still runs the generator with nothing installed). `bitcomposer-player --screenshot out.png --screen 1920x1080 --scale 1.5 --panels settings,mix` renders the window offscreen with a few fresh songs, which is how it was checked here at 1366×768, 1920×1080 at 100/125/150%, 1920×1200 and 4K at 200%; the Windows build renders six setups from the frozen executable on every push (`player-screenshots-windows`). The Windows player is now a folder build (Qt starts at once; a single file would unpack 100 MB per launch) with an icon; the installer installs per user by default, and UPDATE runs it silently and reopens the player.
- The Linux player puts itself in the app menu. pip and pipx run nothing at install time, so `pipx install bitcomposer` left the player reachable only from a terminal until `--install-desktop`; now the first launch of an installed copy writes the menu entry and icon (`player/desktop.py`), and a later launch repairs an entry whose program has moved. An entry the user deletes stays deleted (`--install-desktop` puts it back), one not marked as the player's is left alone, and a checkout run, a frozen build or a Flatpak writes none. `bitcomposer-player --remove-desktop` takes the entry out before `pipx uninstall`. After writing the icon the player tells running KDE programs to look again (the `KIconLoader` signal the icon settings send): the icon's folder is new on a first install, and plasmashell does not see a folder that appears while it runs, so the taskbar showed a "?" until the next login.

- Dead code out, no song changed. Gone: the J-arpeggio harmony writer (retired by the harmony rework), the one-tap echo wrappers, an order-list builder, a periodic-noise voice and a note-name helper nothing called, a pattern packer's unused last-value tables, a Genesis voice parameter nothing passed, the Windows build's launcher for the old generate window, the player's fallback to the old Plasma widget's `~/Music/Chiptune` folder (a saved music folder is kept), and about 200 unused imports, most of them a shared helper list copied into every voice and pack module when `samples` was split. - Media keys and start at login. On Linux the player is an MPRIS service (`org.mpris.MediaPlayer2.bitcomposer`): the desktop sends it the media keys, and the Plasma media applet and lock screen, GNOME's controls and `playerctl` show the track and drive play, pause, next, previous, seek and volume. On Windows the play/pause, next, previous and stop keys are global hot keys. Tested on a private D-Bus (the types, play/pause, seek with Seeked, volume, next, pause not toggling, raise); the Windows keys have not met a real Windows desktop yet.
- The register plan. The lead had gone up an octave with the note-conversion fix (medians 269-320 Hz before, 523-640 after) while the bass and harmony were re-derived downward and the lead was not; by ear it was piercing, and a fifth down was chosen on a listening page. Lowering the lead alone let the cap that held the chord's top voice under the lead's line drag the brass chord back into the band it had been moved out of, so the parts are now placed once per song by `registers.py`, in order: the chord in its family's register, never pushed; the lead in its voice's range (53-76 until a voice has a measured range), its window's floor at most four semitones under the chord's top anchor, its phrases starting above it; the counter-line in the lead's window. The cap is gone. Over the review set the chord registers are unchanged, lead medians sit at 415-480 Hz by sound (were 523-640), and the lead sounds under the chord's top voice in 0-18% of rows. `--lead-shift` and a pack's `lead_shift` habit move the lead's window for comparisons and fine tuning; `info["registers"]` records the plan. Every song with a melody changes.
- Harmony rework (the 1.0.0 blocker). Measured first, on each part rendered alone: a thin harmony was one voice, arpeggiated into a chord with the J effect (pitch cycling every tick), and under every stab sat a per-tick volume slide that had the note silent 250 ms after its attack whatever the cut row said. Strike of the Factory's strings swelled for 100 ms and were gone by 260, warbling: the quack. Now: (1) a thin harmony is two real voices and a full one three; the J arpeggio is retired. The voicing rule keeps nearest-to-anchor voice leading and adds two constraints: no voice doubles a pitch class while a chord tone is missing, and the last voice carries the chord's colour tone (its third, or a sus4's fourth), so a two-voice pad is a tenth and a two-voice brass a sixth or a third, never a bare fifth. (2) A stab holds for its family's length or three times the voice's measured attack (`onset_ms`, new in `generate_instrument`), in rows at the song's tempo, with no slide: brass two rows at 180 BPM, strings most of the bar; it ends on a note-off into the family's release. A struck harmony voice (the orchestra hit, a piano, a vibraphone) never draws the sustain mode. (3) Registers per family: pads, strings and choirs low and wide (lowest voice at C3-D3, 131-147 Hz, the top a tenth up), brass and guitars a close triad at 196-330 Hz, and the top voice capped under the lead's lower quartile in every pattern. (4) Level: every harmony-pool preset was rendered on one chord figure and read with ebur128; the sustaining families land within 1.5 dB of each other, so the family table is flat apart from brass (a 2 dB trim by ear) and the struck voices (+1 dB, measured). The harmony channels drop from 25 to 9 in the written mix: with real voices holding their stabs, 25 put a strings chord's hits at the lead's own level (+1.0 dB, momentary loudness, 95th percentile); the review set now sits 6 to 10 dB under the lead. A hit shorter than the ear's 200 ms integration is written up by the square root of the ratio (a one-row chug at 180 BPM by 1.55, capped at 1.6), so the chug and pulse modes land with the held chords. (5) The counter-line has the third harmony channel to itself and its own level (32 against the lead's 42), with no chord fade on it; it used to take the first channel at the chord's level, decaying like a stab, while the chord voice moved channel and echo pan between patterns. The package list is now discovered as `bitcomposer*`.

- Brass harmony. A lone harmony voice (the thin voicing) was always arpeggiated into a chord with the J effect, the pitch cycling root, fourth, fifth every tick; on a pad that reads as shimmer, on a bright brass it was a rasp, and Northern Dawn chugged that rasp twelve times a second. The J arpeggio now only goes on pad, strings, choir and organ voices; brass, guitars, keys and leads play the plain note. Brass harmony sits a fifth higher (the games stab their brass at 250-580 Hz; ours sat at 160 Hz alone). The Mega Man X synth brass and Mana brass lose their invented bright attacks: the games' samples open on their sustain spectrum. A brass harmony is written 3 dB under a pad's and its echo returns stay under the source whatever the song's stereo mode: bright, in the band the ear weights most, it sat forward even at 14 dB under the kit.
- The whole engine sounded an octave below its labels, and now does not. `midi_to_it_note` subtracted 12 on the belief that IT's C-5 was MIDI 72; IT note 60 is the note that plays a sample at its C5Speed, our samples carry middle C there, so the numbers agree and the offset was wrong. Measured on a rendered note: label 60 played at 130.8 Hz before, 261.6 after. The registers had been tuned by ear around the error, which is why the harmony's lowest voice sat at 39-41 Hz in every sound, under the bass, and why Northern Dawn's brass chug rasped. With the conversion corrected and the bass windows set in MIDI (heavy E1-D#2, 41-78 Hz, a floor at E1), the parts land where they belong: bass 78-107 Hz median, harmony 160-370 Hz, arp 270-340 Hz, lead 570-620 Hz. The kit keeps its pitch (`drums.KIT_NOTE`). Every song changes.
- Bass register raised an octave (superseded by the conversion fix above; the bass floor and the MIDI windows remain). The register windows were set in label space without the pattern's octave shift, so "deep and heavy" at label 36 sounded at 16 Hz, and the octave-bounce and driving styles dropped a twelfth under that: across the sounds a sixth to a half of bass notes sat below 33 Hz, down to 13 Hz. On Northern Dawn (Mega Man X, seed 202) the slap bass pedalled a pedal-push riff at 29 Hz, which sounded like a blown speaker. The windows now start at label 48 (32.7 Hz, a bass guitar's low E), a floor guards every written bass note, and the split between the low and high bass samples moved up with them. Measured over twelve seeds per sound: bass now spans 33 to about 200 Hz with medians between 58 and 77 Hz.
- Harmony mode and voice now agree. A rhythmic harmony mode (chug, pulse, rhythmic, ostinato) redraws a slow-attack harmony voice (strings, pad, choir families) from the pool's fast ones, and under a rhythmic mode the contrast rule no longer exempts pads. Northern Dawn (Mega Man X, seed 202) put a chug gallop at 180 BPM on the strings: 264 of 266 chord notes were shorter than the voice's 96 ms swell, under an organ lead and a square arp sustaining in the same octave. Over sixty Mega Man X seeds the pairing happened in eighteen; it now happens in none where the pool has a fast voice.
- Song length clamped to a band. Over sixty random seeds the median was two minutes with a tail each way: three in ten under 1:30 (a short form at speed; Glade of Fire ran 43 seconds) and two in ten over 3:00 (a long form with its AABA repeated at 80 BPM, up to six minutes). Under 75 seconds the body plays twice, as a level loop would, then the last section again if still short; over 210 seconds the latest repeat of a section group is dropped (a through-composed song loses its last section), the first of every group, the intro and the ending kept. Orphaned patterns are compacted out of the file. The spread stays (median 2.1 min); the tails are gone. `info["length_sec"]` and `info["length_adjust"]` say what happened.
- Widget playlist: the list grows with the folder up to twenty-four rows and scrolls past that, and it keeps the playing track in view. It used to stop at 200 px with no scrollbar, so from the ninth track on a song was in the folder and in the daemon's playlist but not on screen, the playing one included. The same seed under two sounds no longer shares a title (the sound salts the title's seed).
- Titles read the song. The scale still sets the mood of the adjectives, and now the energy and tempo add their words (a chill song can be a Lullaby or a Vigil, an intense one a Siege or a Pursuit, a fast one a Flight), and the sound sets the places: a Mega Man X song is set in a Fortress, a Hangar or a Reactor Core, an FFVI song in an Opera House, a Cathedral or on the Veldt, a Mana song in a Grove, a Spring or the Pure Land, an Evermore song in a Pyramid, a Jungle or the Tar Pits. Eight patterns over lists of thirty to sixty words. The old single pattern gave 720 titles per mood and repeated a name every thirty-odd songs; the new ones give about thirteen thousand distinct titles per sound and mood and a first repeat around two hundred songs. Titles of existing seeds change.
