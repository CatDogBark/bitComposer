# SPC import: a game's own instruments and habits, translated

Plan, written 2026-10-18; nothing here is built yet. The goal is a pack
made from a game's real sound files: point the player at a soundtrack's
`.spc` files and get a SOUND that plays the game's own samples, with the
habits of its score (tempo, registers, how the harmony moves, the drums,
the echo), measured rather than guessed. In short, a translator from the
SNES sound system to bitComposer's.

Two halves. **Import** reads what a snapshot holds outright: the sample
bank, the envelopes, the echo. **Emulation** runs the game's own music
driver to find out what the snapshot only implies: which notes are
played, on which voice, when. The import is proven ground (every built-in
pack was made from it, by hand); the emulation is the new and large part.

Out of scope: getting the files. The importer takes `.spc` files as they
are; finding them, and unpacking `.rsn` archives (RAR), is the user's
business.

## What an SPC file is

A snapshot of the SNES sound subsystem at one instant, 66 KB:

- **The 64 KB of audio RAM**, which holds the game's music driver (SPC700
  code), the song's sequence data in that driver's own format, and the
  sample bank: BRR-compressed samples (9-byte blocks of 16 samples, four
  prediction filters, end and loop flags) found through a directory whose
  page is the DSP's `DIR` register, four bytes per sample (start, loop
  start).
- **The 128 DSP registers**: per voice (eight) the volume, pitch (14 bits,
  `0x1000` plays a sample at 32 kHz as recorded), source number (`SRCN`),
  ADSR or GAIN envelope; globally key on and off (`KON`, `KOFF`), the echo
  (`EON` which voices send, `EDL` delay in 16 ms steps, `EFB` feedback, the
  eight FIR taps, `EVOL`), noise (`NON`, the clock in `FLG`) and pitch
  modulation (`PMON`).
- **The SPC700 CPU registers** (PC, A, X, Y, PSW, SP) and the timer and
  I/O state at `0xF0`-`0xFF`, so execution can resume exactly where the
  dump stopped. An ID666 tag gives the title, the game, and often the
  song's length and fade.

Rips are usually dumped at the start of a song, so running the snapshot
plays the song from its top.

## What exists already

The research tools behind the four packs (`~/diags/bitcomposer/*-spc/tools`,
not in the repository) do the import half by hand:

- `survey.py` decodes every BRR sample across a set of snapshots,
  de-duplicates by decoded PCM, and profiles each (steady-state harmonics,
  centroid, attack, decay, loop).
- `voices.py` reads, per snapshot, which voice plays each sample, at what
  sounding pitch, with what envelope, with echo or not.
- `fine.py` and `match.py` compare a sample's profile with a synthesised
  voice's.
- Echo per pack was read off the DSP registers across the snapshots.

What they could not do, and what limited every pack: a snapshot is one
instant. It shows eight voices mid-song and says nothing about which one
is the melody (doublings, trills and chord tops pulled the traced "lead"
medians up by an octave or more; see the register plan in ROADMAP.md),
nor about tempo, phrase, groove or form. The rest of the song is in the
sequence data, in a format that differs per driver (Square's, Nintendo's,
Capcom's...). Reading every driver's format is endless; running the
driver is one job.

## What others have built (researched 2026-10-18)

Before writing an emulator, what exists. In this plan the **SNES chip** is
the S-DSP (it plays the voices, the samples, the echo) and the **SNES
sound CPU** is the SPC700 (it runs the game's music driver, which tells
the SNES chip what to play). Nothing beyond the SPC file is needed to run
a driver: the sound unit's only built-in code is a 64-byte boot ROM, and
the driver itself is in every snapshot.

- **Tests for an SNES sound CPU core.**
  [SingleStepTests](https://github.com/SingleStepTests/ProcessorTests)
  (`spc700/`; the sister repository
  [SingleStepTests/spc700](https://github.com/SingleStepTests/spc700) is
  MIT): 1,000 JSON cases for each of the 256 instructions, each with the
  registers and RAM before and after and every bus cycle. Generated by
  another emulator (JSMoo), so a strong cross-check rather than ground
  truth. [gilyon/snes-tests](https://github.com/gilyon/snes-tests) (MIT):
  test ROMs for the SNES sound CPU run on real hardware, with every test's
  inputs and expected outputs listed: the hardware truth, timing included.
  Together they check a core instruction by instruction without a single
  game file.
- **Reference emulators.** blargg's `snes_spc` (LGPL 2.1; in Game_Music_Emu,
  forks such as [yupferris/snes_spc](https://github.com/yupferris/snes_spc)
  and a [D port](https://github.com/Herringway/spc700)) emulates the SNES
  sound CPU and the SNES chip accurately, but has no hook that logs what the
  driver writes, which is the one thing this plan needs. No Python
  emulator of either exists.
- **Converters by emulation.**
  [spc2it](https://github.com/uyjulian/spc2it) converts SPC to IT: Brad
  Martin's OpenSPC 0.301 as improved by Dwedit, on SNEeSe's SNES sound CPU
  core. It catches each key-on through a callback on SNES chip writes,
  turns the voice's pitch into the nearest IT note against the sample's
  base frequency, and writes one IT row per update tick (speed 1, the tempo
  set from the update rate): a recording on a time grid, faithful in timing
  but with no beat grid, which is the step this plan's translator adds.
  Its files carry their own terms (SNEeSe's Clarified Artistic License,
  GPL-compatible; OpenSPC asks for credit and that its author be told);
  the repository has no top-level license file. spc2midi and
  [spc2midi-tsuu](https://vgmrips.net/forum/viewtopic.php?p=19957) do SPC
  to MIDI the same way. These prove the approach.
- **Converters by reading the music data.**
  [VGMTrans](https://github.com/vgmtrans/vgmtrans) (zlib) reads songs
  straight from the driver's sequence data, with no emulation, for 15
  SNES driver families: exact notes, tempo and loops where it knows the
  format. Of this plan's test games it covers FFVI and Secret of Mana
  (`AkaoSnes`) and Mega Man X (`CapcomSnes`); Secret of Evermore is not
  among them. It does not do pitch slides on SPC, per the spc2midi-tsuu
  author.

Copies of spc2it and VGMTrans are kept as research material outside the
repository (`~/diags/bitcomposer/research/`).

### Decided (2026-10-18)

1. **Emulation, for any SPC file, with VGMTrans as a checker.** The
   importer runs whatever driver a snapshot holds, so it takes any SPC
   file; there is no per-game or per-driver code in it. The games named in
   this plan are the test set that checks it, not a limit on what it
   imports. Its correctness rests on what is driver-independent: the SNES
   sound CPU core passing every instruction test and hardware test. On top
   of that, for test games VGMTrans reads, its MIDI of the same snapshot is
   a note-for-note answer to compare the translator with; for the rest
   (Evermore here), spc2it's recording and reference audio. VGMTrans's own
   approach, a reader per driver, is not followed: it covers only the
   drivers someone has written a reader for.
2. **The emulator is Rust** (decided 2026-10-18, from the prototype
   below). The generator, the player, the reader and the translator stay
   Python; only the emulator is compiled. Rust over C because SPC files
   come from the internet: they are untrusted input, and Rust is memory
   safe at C's speed. PyO3 and maturin build one wheel per platform that
   serves every Python version.
3. **Research material versus our code.** Anything can be read, run and
   learned from. Code that goes into bitComposer must be under a license
   that fits an MIT project (MIT, BSD, zlib), with credit; code under
   LGPL, Artistic or no stated license is studied, not pasted: the
   emulator is written from the documentation and from understanding. What
   we learned from is credited here.

## The design: sources, chip decoders, one translator (decided 2026-10-18)

The import is three layers, so that every console after the SNES reuses
the last one:

1. **A source per file format** turns a file into a log of timed writes to
   a sound chip's registers. The file says what it is in its first bytes,
   and each format belongs to one console, so there is no guessing which
   hardware a log came from. `.spc` (`SNES-SPC700 Sound File Data`) runs
   the game's driver on the SNES sound CPU emulator and logs what it writes
   to the SNES chip. `.vgm` (`Vgm `, whose header lists its chips and their
   clocks) already is a register log, recorded from the console when the
   file was made: no emulation. `.nsf` (`NESM`, the NES) would run a NES CPU
   emulator, and so on.
2. **A chip decoder per sound chip** knows what its registers mean (the
   SNES chip's key-on, pitch and sample number; the Genesis YM2612's key-on,
   frequency and patch) and turns its log into events common to every
   chip: a note started on a voice, with an instrument, at a pitch and a
   volume, at a time; a note released; an echo or a pan changed.
3. **The translator**, shared, turns events into the beat grid, notes,
   roles and habits, and the outputs (the one-to-one `.it`, the pack). It
   never knows which console the music came from.

A log is only as true as its source. For VGM it was recorded from the
real sound chip. For SPC it is what the emulator's SNES sound CPU wrote,
which equals what the real SNES chip received exactly when the emulator
behaves as the real SNES sound CPU does: the instruction tests and the
hardware test ROMs are what make that a checked claim. A new console is a
new source and chip decoder; everything from the events on comes free.

## The pieces

### 1. The pack file format (built: `docs/PACKS.md`)

The "your own packs" roadmap item: a pack as data, not Python. A pack
file is JSON holding the table a built-in pack keeps as `PACK` (palette,
label, voices per role, habits, trims), with the pack's own voices as WAV
files (tuning, loop, release family, volume). `bitcomposer/packs/packfile.py`
checks it, registers its voices with the sample registry and the pack in
`PACKS` (not in the random draw), and `--pack-file` composes with it; the
player's PACKS panel will pass that same option. It carries every habit
the composer reads (27) and every role.

Changed from the first plan: the built-in packs stay Python. Their
tables carry comments recording where each number came from (bank
counts, DSP readings), which JSON cannot hold. Instead a test writes each
built-in pack in the format and reads it back unchanged, which proves
the format can say everything they say and keeps it that way.

### 2. The SPC reader (built: `bitcomposer/spc/`)

The research tools' decoding, made a module with tests: the file layout,
ID666 (text and binary), the BRR decoder, the sample directory, the DSP
register map (per voice the volume, pitch, sample and envelope; the echo,
noise and pitch modulation). Pure Python, standard library only, like the
generator. `python -m bitcomposer.spc` shows what a snapshot holds, and
`--export DIR` writes every sample of a set of snapshots once, as WAV, with
its loop in `samples.json`.

Three things the research tools did not need and an import does:

- **The chip's arithmetic exactly.** The research decoder ran the filters
  at half scale and clipped where the chip wraps: a sum past 15 bits loses
  its top bit on the SNES, which flips its sign. The reader wraps, as the
  chip does; a random-block test checks it against a second decoder
  written the way bsnes does it.
- **Loops as they sound on the repeats.** A loop whose first block
  predicts from the samples before it decodes differently on its first
  pass and on the repeats, so a tracker looping the first pass clicks.
  The reader appends repeat passes until one decodes as the last did
  (within 64 of 65536, where the filters' rounding drifts for ever) and
  loops that one.
- **Telling samples from leftovers.** A snapshot's directory has 256
  entries, most pointing at whatever the RAM holds now. An entry counts as
  a sample when every block's shift is at most 12 and the first block is
  unfiltered, as encoders write them, or when a voice is playing it.

### 3. The emulator (Rust)

The SNES sound CPU, the timers, and the SNES chip, running the game's
driver and recording what it tells the SNES chip:

- **The SPC700 CPU**: 256 opcodes over a small set of addressing modes, at
  1.024 MHz, cycle-counted so the timers run true.
- **The three timers** (two at 8 kHz, one at 64 kHz). Drivers pace their
  tempo off them, so they are the clock everything is measured by.
- **The SNES chip**, logging every write with its cycle time (`KON`,
  `KOFF`, pitch, `SRCN`, volume, envelope, echo, noise), and emulated, not
  only written to: drivers read its live state back. Secret of Evermore's
  driver polls every voice's envelope level (`ENVX`) and the end flags
  (`ENDX`) about 50 times a second, values that exist only if the voices
  run, sample by sample: the pitch counter walking each BRR sample, the
  ADSR and GAIN envelopes, the end flags, and the echo, which writes into
  RAM a driver can read. So the SNES chip is emulated as accurately as the
  SNES sound CPU, short of mixing an output nobody listens to.
- **The I/O ports** the main CPU would talk through, left as the snapshot
  has them (a running song does not wait for the game).
- **The IPL ROM** (64 bytes) for drivers that jump into it.

How long to run: the ID666 length when the tag has one, otherwise until
the song's register writes repeat (the loop point), capped at a few
minutes of song time.

**The prototype that chose the language** (2026-10-18; kept outside the
repository). A complete SNES sound CPU core in plain Python, with the sound
unit's memory map, timers, boot ROM and the SNES chip register log, passed
all 254,000 SingleStepTests cases (the two halting instructions aside,
which drivers do not use) and ran real songs from all four test games.
Measured on a laptop (Core i9-10885H):

- the SNES sound CPU alone: 1.1-1.2 million instructions a second, about
  250,000 per second of song, so 4.3 to 5.0 times real time;
- a bare SNES chip voice model alone (no interpolation, a simple
  envelope): 5.3 times real time, 0.74 us per voice per sample;
- both, with the SNES chip accurate: about 1.5 to 2.5 times real time;
- blargg's compiled emulator (libgme, with full sound synthesis): about
  170 times real time on FFVI's battle theme.

Importing a soundtrack runs every song to its loop point: FFVI's 82
songs would take tens of minutes to over an hour in Python and about a
minute compiled. Tuning the Python buys perhaps twice; the gap is nearly
a hundred. Hence Rust. The Python prototype stays useful as a second
opinion: the same snapshot through both, logs compared, while the Rust
emulator is written.

**How it ships.** The emulator becomes a second, compiled package
published from this repository, with wheels built per platform when a
release is tagged (Linux, Windows, macOS) and bundled by the Windows
build. It is optional where no wheel exists: the generator and the player
install everywhere, and IMPORT says what is missing there.

The core is checked instruction by instruction against the SingleStepTests
cases and the gilyon hardware tests before it runs a song.

**Built so far** (`emu/`): the SNES sound CPU, every instruction, passing
all 254,000 SingleStepTests cases; the sound unit around it (RAM, the I/O
registers, the three timers kept lazily, the boot ROM) with the SNES chip
as a register file and every write logged. On real songs from the four
test games it runs at about 1,000 to 1,180 times real time (285 million
instructions a second on a Ryzen 7 9800X3D), and its log is identical to
the Python prototype's: the same writes, the same key-ons at the same
cycles.

Then the SNES chip (`emu/src/dsp.rs`), written from fullsnes: the eight
voices walking their BRR samples with the hardware's Gaussian
interpolation (its 512-entry table and overflow behaviour), pitch
modulation and noise; the ADSR and GAIN envelopes; KON and KOFF polled
every other sample with the 5-sample key-on delay; ENDX; the echo with
its FIR filter and feedback, written into RAM; the mixer. It steps one
sample every 32 CPU cycles, so the driver reads live state. Voices the
driver has not keyed on since the snapshot wait silent, since an SPC file
does not say where a voice was in its sample. With the SNES chip and its
output on, songs run at about 200 to 230 times real time.

Checked against libgme's rendering of the same snapshots (evidence, not a
verdict: it is another emulator): FFVI's battle theme correlates at 0.98
(stretches at 0.998 to 1.000, aligned to the sample), Secret of Mana's at
0.93. Secret of Evermore's cannot line up sample for sample: its driver
plays the noise generator constantly, and the noise sequence's position is
not in an SPC file either. Two causes on our side were tested and ruled
out (when ENDX is set; whether the snapshot's KON keys voices on); the
driver's key-ons came out identical in every run. The snapshot's KON did
matter elsewhere, though. Most snapshots are taken as a song's first notes
key on, before the SNES chip has started them, and libgme keys those voices
on as it loads the file; the emulator left them silent until the driver
keyed them again (half a second at the median, never for a held note: 1343
of 1836 songs were missing opening or held notes, in the emulator and so in
their modules). Since 0.3.1 it keys them on too, and the decoder makes them
notes from the start: against libgme over 156 songs' first 10 s, the
loudness contours correlate at a median of 1.000, the level matches to
within 1 % in 80 % of them (libgme plays 1.4 times louder, its own gain, and
skips a track's opening silence). Listened to side by
side on FFVI, Mana and two Evermore songs: identical and accurate, the
only difference that libgme stops at the length in the file's tag.

### 4. The translator

In stages, with what is decided: `TRANSLATOR.md`.

The SNES chip decoder turns the register log into events (the design,
above), and the shared translator turns events into musical facts, then
measurements.

**Built so far:** the emulator is called from Python
(`bitcomposer_emu.run`, wrapped by `bitcomposer/spc/emulate.py`), which
hands back every SNES chip write. `bitcomposer/spc/notes.py`, the SNES
chip decoder, replays them into notes: the voice, the sample, the pitch,
the volume and envelope at key-on, the bends and swells while it sounds,
and its end (key-off, the voice's next key-on, or the soft reset). A
key-on while the voice's KOFF bit is still set counts if KOFF clears before
the SNES chip's next poll, as the hardware has it: Factor 5's driver keys
voices off and on in one go and clears KOFF just after, and every Super
Turrican 2 song converted silent until then. A note keyed at pitch 0 takes
the pitch written just after (EarthBound sweeps up from 0).
`bitcomposer/spc/tuning.py` measures each sample's own pitch (a loop's
whole cycles; otherwise autocorrelation past the lag where the wave has
moved on from itself, the peak placed between whole lags), so a note has
a name: FFVI's battle bass is an A1 ostinato, Secret of Mana's opening an
F major chord, every instrument of it within a cent of the same offset
(-8 cents: the game's own tuning, which the translator will calibrate
out). `python -m bitcomposer.spc --notes FILE.spc` lists them.

**SPC to IT** (`bitcomposer/spc/convert.py`, with `snes_chip.py` for the
SNES chip's sound and `it_song.py` for the IT song; `python -m
bitcomposer.spc --to-it OUT.it FILE.spc`): the notes as an IT module in
instrument mode, so a converted song opens in a tracker as a tracker
song. One IT sample
per sample played, found by the addresses the SNES chip read as it keyed
each voice on (Secret of Evermore's driver writes a voice's directory
entry just before keying it; its snapshot's directory is placeholders), its
C5Speed set so the notes fall on whole semitones (and whole octaves off
where that brings more of them onto IT's keys, 1-119: a cell cannot write
C-0, and DKC2's bass, five octaves under its sample's rate, fell silent in
stretches), and filtered as the
SNES chip's Gaussian interpolation colours it (a lowpass in the sample's
own terms, -4 dB at a quarter of its rate and -15 dB at the top, where an
IT player interpolates almost ideally); one instrument per sample and
envelope setting, its volume envelope simulated from ADSR or GAIN (each
tick holding its window's RMS, so a fast decay keeps the energy of its
peak); bends as fine slides row by row; key-offs as cuts.
A note whose envelope registers are written while it sounds plays an
instrument without an envelope, its envelope simulated through the writes
and carried whole in the volume column, and is cut where it falls silent:
Mega Man X's driver keys every note on held and releases it with a GAIN
fade a moment before the next, never with a key-off; Secret of Evermore's
shapes every note itself with hundreds of GAIN ramps and holds, reading
the envelope back to know when to switch, so the simulation steps where
the SNES chip's shared rate counter does (counted from each note's start
instead, it drifted a step on most switches: the wind's level 2.17 times
the original's, 1.70 on the counter, 0.95 with the interpolation's
softness in the samples too). Volumes side for
side: the SNES chip sets each side's level on its own, an IT player pans
linearly (a centred note at half its volume each side), so a note's IT
volume is its two sides' sum and its pan the right side's share (followed
row by row where the driver moves it while the note sounds: EarthBound
pans held notes slowly from side to side); the SNES chip's master volume is
the IT global volume, its changes (a driver's fade-out) on a channel of
their own, and the two traded where one has room, since a note's loudness
is their product and both are whole numbers: the channels up and the global
volume down as far as the loudest voice allows (IT's volumes are coarse at
the bottom: EarthBound's quiet voices rounded to silence), or the other way
where voices pass IT's 64 under a low master (DKC2's 123 under 60); the song runs as an SPC player plays
it, the tag's length and then its fade, and the module ends on its last
row (rounded up to a whole pattern, held notes rang on past the end with
their echo stacked, Dark World twice as loud). Noise plays the SNES chip's own
noise, its 15-bit shift register rendered at the output rate for each
noise clock a song uses, each value held as the hardware holds it, each
note starting somewhere else in it. The echo as repeats: each note
sending to the echo again on a channel of its own, an echo delay late (its
copy carrying what the delays leave over a whole row as silence, so the
repeat lands between rows, and its sign against the note: the echo
volume's times the master volume's on each side, and the feedback's to
the k-1; rounded to rows and unsigned, repeats stacked by chance on held
tones, and Seiken Densetsu 3's, against its notes on one side, added),
until the repeats fall to -30 dB (as many as IT's 64 channels allow,
shared among the voices that send; three stopped Dark World's 1.1 s tail
at 0.24 s), raised past the master volume (the SNES chip leaves the echo
out of it, an IT global volume reaches every channel), played from a copy of its sample as the echo hands it back after that many trips round its loop (the filter once a trip: its colour, and its span, which rows alone leave out: Dark World's repeats came 7 samples a trip early and stacked a third brighter), coloured by the echo
filter (at the pitch its echoing notes mostly have) and scaled by the echo
volume, the feedback and the filter's gain over the note itself (an echo
of delay 0 is no echo: its buffer holds a sample, so it is a loop of a
sample through the filter and the feedback, a tone control on every voice
that sends, and plays as one copy of each note at its own row, coloured by
every trip round the loop: Flammie Coming's lows were at half without it);
when the
two echo volumes have opposite signs (Mega Man X's widening trick), a
voice near the middle has its repeats play in surround, one side inverted
(surround plays centred: a panned voice's echo there landed on the wrong
side, 2.46 times its dry level on one and 0.12 on the other, against the
original's 0.33 on both). Pitch modulation (a
voice's pitch bent by the output of the voice below it, at audio rate: an
FM timbre, or in The Phantom Train a tone shaken into a hiss by a noise
voice) has no IT equivalent, so a pitch-modulated note is kept as the
emulator played it: the emulator records the voice's own output (after
its envelope, before its volumes), and notes played alike share one
recording, the longest, on the keyboard of an instrument of recordings.
10 of FFVI's 82 songs and 8 of Mana's 69 use it, none of Evermore's or
Mega Man X's; the most recordings any of them needs is 28 (FFVI's New
Continent). Rows are the driver's tick when its key-ons land on a running
timer IT can run, and keep pace with through the song (Evermore's 50 Hz,
DKC2's 80), else IT's finest grid, 100 a second (FFVI's and Mana's drivers
key on anywhere in a tick; Mega Man X's 125 Hz is past IT's 102; IT's tempo
comes in steps of 0.4 rows a second, and Factor 5's 62.5 Hz on 62.4 rows
fell a tenth of a second behind over a song).

`drivers/` records every game tested so far by music driver (a file per
driver family), with the latest scores and what each driver does. Measured against the
emulator's own output (`tools/spc_check.py`;
loudness contours, 50 ms windows over the first 14 s, each side,
averaged; the level is the IT's RMS over the emulator's): FFVI's battle theme 0.98 (level 0.99) and The
Phantom Train 0.97 (0.90), Mana's Together Always 0.96 (0.98) and The
Oracle 0.98 (1.01), Evermore's main title 0.98 (1.01) and Southern Jungle
0.94 (1.10), Mega Man X's Sigma Intro 0.84 (0.95) and Spark Mandrill 0.95
(0.99). The emulator can leave voices out of its output, so the same
comparison runs voice by voice, which is how most of these were found:
Spark Mandrill's melody went from 0.54 to 0.94 when the envelope writes
were carried; Southern Jungle's wind was silent (a GAIN level of 16 in
2047, which rounds to nothing in an IT envelope) until those notes played
their envelope in the volume column, 0.56 to 0.94; Mana's songs played at
half their level until volumes went side for side. The Phantom Train's
pitch-modulated voice was a steady beep (its spectrum's correlation with
the emulator's -0.01); recorded, it is the hiss it should be (0.88). An
echo copied at full range is worse than none where the filter passes
little bass (Spark Mandrill 0.86 against 0.93), which is why the repeats
are coloured: they then match the original's band levels without adding
bass. Twenty more games (862 songs, twelve more drivers) found six more of the
SNES chip's ways that the converter now carries, listed with the driver
that showed each in `drivers/snes/README.md`; over all 1108 songs scored,
the median gap is 0.46 dB, 1.00 at the 90th percentile.

For the SNES chip:

- **Notes.** A `KON` on a voice is a note: its sample (`SRCN` at that
  moment), its pitch (the sample's fundamental, measured once by the
  reader, times pitch / `0x1000`, to a MIDI note and cents), its volume and
  pan, its length (to the `KOFF` or to where the envelope reaches zero).
  Pitch slides and vibrato show up as pitch writes between notes.
- **Tempo and grid.** Note onsets against the timer clock give the beat
  and the rows; swing shows up as a regular long-short offset.
- **Roles.** From the notes, not the snapshot: drums are the unpitched or
  fixed-pitch percussive samples on the grid; the bass is the lowest
  sustained line; the melody is the line with the most varied, phrase-shaped
  motion in the upper register; harmony is notes sounding together under
  it; the arp is fast repeated chord tones. Each guess carries its
  evidence, and the user can overrule it.
- **Habits.** The translated song measured with the same yardsticks the
  generator is judged by (ANALYSIS.md): tempo, scale and key motion,
  energy and density, harmony mode (held, stabbed, chugged), bass style,
  drum density and groove, echo settings and which roles send. Over a
  whole soundtrack they become the pack's weighted habits.

### 5. Two outputs

- **SPC to IT, one to one.** The translated song written as an `.it`
  module with the decoded samples: the same notes on the same samples.
  This is the translator's proof: render the `.it` with libopenmpt, render
  the `.spc` with a reference player, compare onsets, pitch and loudness
  (below). It is also a feature in its own right (a game's song in a
  tracker), for the user's own files.
- **A pack.** The soundtrack's samples as the pack's voices (envelopes
  baked and loops kept, the way `samples.dsp` finishes the SNES voices),
  the roles from the translator with the user's corrections, the habits
  measured over every song. The generator composes new songs with it,
  as with any built-in pack; it is chosen in the player's PACKS panel
  (below), kept apart from the built-in sounds.

### 6. The player: PACKS

Imported packs get their own place, apart from the built-in sounds and
SET (decided 2026-10-18):

- **A PACKS tile** after MIX in the tool row (AUTO, SET, MIX, PACKS),
  opening a panel inside the window the way SET and MIX do. Room for it
  comes from the GENERATE button, which can shorten from
  "GENERATE NEW TRACK" to "GENERATE"; checked at the narrowest screens
  the window is checked at.
- **The list.** One row per imported pack: its name, the game (from the
  files' ID666 tags), how many songs it was measured from, and USE,
  ROLES (the role picker again) and DELETE.
- **IMPORT** at the bottom of the panel: choose `.spc` files or a folder;
  a progress screen over the window while each song is emulated, the way
  the update screen shows its work ("emulating song 3 of 24"); then the
  role picker (hear each sample, see what the translator decided and why,
  change it); then the pack joins the list.
- **USE** makes the pack the sound for GENERATE and auto-generate. One
  sound is in use at a time: SET's SOUND row then reads
  "imported: <name>", and choosing a built-in sound there switches back.
- **Not in random.** SOUND's random draw never picks an imported pack
  (decided 2026-10-18; an "include in random" switch can come later if
  it is missed).
- **Songs** from an imported pack are songs like any other: the mixer,
  KEEP and the ten-song prune apply. The song message names the pack,
  and a song keeps playing after its pack is deleted, since its samples
  are inside the `.it` file.

Imported packs live in the user's settings folder.

## Checking each step

Measured, as everything here is:

- **Reader (done):** over the 258 snapshots the four packs were built
  from, the 4,728 samples the research tools found decode to exactly twice
  their values in 3,414; the other 1,313 differ only where the research
  decoder let a sum run past 15 bits, which the chip wraps. Of the 262
  samples the voices were playing, the plausibility test keeps 259 (all
  262 are listed, being played) and drops 70% of the directory's other
  entries. 56 of the 61 played loops that never repeat exactly settle
  within the seam tolerance.
- **Emulator:** every SingleStepTests case (done: all 254,000) and every
  gilyon hardware test passes; then the onsets in its key-on log line up with the onsets in
  the reference player's audio of the same snapshot, within a few ms, for
  every driver in the test set (the four built-in packs' games: three
  Square titles, whose drivers differ by version, and Capcom's Mega Man X). A reference player is a development
  tool only (for example ffmpeg built with libgme), never a dependency.
- **Translator:** for FFVI, Secret of Mana and Mega Man X, its notes
  against VGMTrans's MIDI of the same snapshot, note for note (onset,
  pitch, length, which voice); for every game, the one-to-one `.it` against
  the reference render: onset match, pitch error in cents, loudness
  envelope correlation.
- **Habits:** a ground truth already exists. The four built-in packs were
  tuned by hand from these same games; importing each game's snapshots
  should land the measured habits near the hand-made `PACK` tables (FFVI's
  echo is 80 ms with feedback 0.64 in every snapshot, for one). Where they
  disagree, either the translator or the hand tuning is wrong, and the
  numbers say which to look at.
- **Determinism:** a song from an imported pack is a function of the seed,
  the version and the pack's files, like any other.

## Order

Each step is its own branch and pull request, shippable on its own:

1. The pack file format (built), proven on the built-in packs by a
   round-trip test.
2. The SPC reader (built), checked against the research tools.
3. The emulator, tested against reference renders. The long one.
4. The translator and SPC to IT, which proves 3.
5. The pack from a soundtrack, checked against the built-in packs.
6. The player's PACKS panel: the list, IMPORT, the role picker, USE.

## Risks

- **Emulator accuracy.** Drivers lean on timer behaviour and odd
  instructions; one wrong flag stalls a song. The test set has four
  drivers; others will find new faults.
- **Packaging a compiled core**: wheels on every platform from CI, the
  Windows build bundling it, and a clear message where no wheel exists.
  (Speed itself is settled: Rust.)
- **Drivers that load samples during the song** (from the main CPU): a
  snapshot holds only what was loaded when it was taken; such songs
  import with what was there.
- **Pitch modulation, noise and echo tricks** some scores rely on:
  logged and translated where IT can say it, reported where it cannot.
- **Role guesses** will be wrong sometimes; the role picker exists for
  that.

## The files are the user's

The repository ships the code, never game files or anything extracted
from them: the samples are the publishers' recordings. Imported packs
and the songs made with them contain those samples, so they stay on the
user's machine. A future "share a pack" feature leaves imported samples
out. The built-in packs stay as they are, re-synthesised from
measurements, borrowing nothing.
